Apple accessibility · Voice Control

It doesn't guess what you mean. It gives everything a name.

Voice Control is not Siri with a bigger vocabulary. It is a pointer and a keyboard made of words, and it rests on one idea: before you can act on a thing, the thing needs a name. Everything below is a way of getting one.

Six parts, two and a half minutes, spoken aloud

It isn't Siri

Siri

Takes a request and decides what to do about it. You describe an outcome; something else picks the steps. When it misreads you, you get the wrong outcome.

Voice Control

Takes a command and performs it, exactly, on whatever is in front of you. You pick the steps. When it misreads you, you say the step again.

Apple shipped it in 2019, in macOS Catalina and iOS 13, and the recognition runs on the device. After the one-time download, it works with the network switched off.

The naming ladder

Three ways to name a thing, ordered by how much the app has already done for you. You climb down only as far as you need — and the rungs below never run out, which is the point.

The label it already has

Buttons, links and menu items are named by whatever they say — or by the accessibility label behind them, which is how an icon with no text still answers to Compose.

A number, handed out on request

Every control on screen puts on a badge. Nothing needs a label, or even a sensible one — two buttons both called Open stop being ambiguous the moment they are 7 and 12.

A coordinate, when there is no control at all

The screen is cut into numbered squares. Say a number and that square becomes a grid of its own. Two or three steps in you are pointing at one pixel of a photo, inside an app that never labelled anything.

Lost? Show me what to say lists the commands that work right here, in this app, on this screen — the one command worth memorising. And if you would rather not ask each time, Settings keeps names, numbers or the grid on screen permanently.

Dictation is just another mode

Inside a text field your words become text, and the editing commands keep working on the text you just made. Selection, replacement and correction are all spoken, so your hands never arrive.

You said I'll be going tomorrow and I'll bring the slides
After “replace going tomorrow with going to Denver” I'll be going to Denver and I'll bring the slides

Which leaves the interesting problem: how does it know delete that is a command and not two words you wanted typed? It doesn't reason about it. A phrase it recognises as a command is treated as a command, every time — and when one particular phrase keeps stealing your sentences, you switch that single command off in Settings and keep the rest.

Fingers, supplied

A touchscreen expects fingers. Voice Control supplies them — and because the gestures compose with the grid, one can begin at square 19 and end wherever you say.

Make it answer to you

Custom commands

Bind a phrase you choose to an action, a Shortcut, or a run of keystrokes — scoped to one app if you like. This is where a five-step routine collapses into four syllables.

Vocabulary

Teach it the words it would otherwise fight you on: surnames, product names, the spelling your team insists on. Recognition is only as good as the list of things it expects to hear.

Sleep and wake

When you need it to stop listening, say so. After that it listens for one phrase only, which is the whole safety story in two commands.

Who it is held to

Voice Control was built for people who cannot use a touchscreen or a trackpad, and it is held to that standard rather than to convenience. Every part of the interface has to be reachable by voice alone — which is why the grid exists at all, and why the overlay can be left on screen for good.

The rest follows from taking that seriously. On an iPhone with Face ID it can stop listening the moment you look away. Recognition happens on the device, and your audio only reaches Apple if you opt in to Improve Assistive Voice Features. What you are left with is an operating system you can drive with a sentence — and, once you have driven it that way for an afternoon, a fairly sharp opinion about which apps ever bothered to label their buttons.

Every phrase on this page — the six narrations and each spoken command — was rendered by Kyutai Pocket TTS (voice alba) on a laptop CPU at about five and a half times real time, then shipped with the page. Nothing is synthesised in your browser and nothing is fetched while you read.
